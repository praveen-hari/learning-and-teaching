    Unit 2: Teaching in Diverse Classrooms and Learning In and Out of School¶

    Introduction¶

    This unit addresses two interconnected realities of education:

    1. Classrooms are diverse — students differ in abilities, backgrounds, and learning needs
    2. Learning extends beyond school — meaningful education happens in many settings

    These concepts are connected: understanding diversity helps teachers see that different students may learn better in different environments. A child struggling in a traditional classroom might excel when learning through observation in a real-world setting. Together, these ideas shape how we design complete educational experiences.

    Part A: Teaching in Diverse Classrooms¶

    2.01 Concept of Diversity¶

    Diversity means a group with variety or dissimilar elements. The term is interpreted according to context:

    Context What Diversity Means
    Social Diversity People in a community differ in caste, religion, race, language, locality, economic status, occupation
    Biodiversity Different organisms classified as plants, animals, insects, reptiles, birds based on physical forms
    Classroom Diversity Students differ in intelligence, language ability, interests, aptitudes, learning style, socio-economic status

    Why this matters: Just as biodiversity makes ecosystems resilient, classroom diversity enriches the learning environment — but it also requires teachers to adapt their approaches.

    2.02 Meaning and Definition of Diverse Classrooms¶

    Students in any classroom differ in physical and psychological traits as well as behaviour:

    • Gifted children who grasp concepts quickly
    • Average learners who form the majority
    • Slow learners or those with learning disabilities
    • Students from marginalized sections (weaker sections, minority communities, tribals, scheduled castes)
    • Students from different castes, religions, and mother tongues

    Definition

    A Diverse Classroom is one in which students significantly differ in their physical and psychological traits, socio-economic status, and learning abilities. In such classrooms, teaching-learning activities must also be diverse.

    The connection: Because students are diverse, teaching methods cannot be uniform. This leads directly to the concept of differentiated instruction discussed later.

    2.03 Types of Diversity in Classrooms¶

    Some diversity is obvious (race, dress, language). Other diversity is invisible (learning styles, motivation, beliefs). A responsive teacher must recognize and provide for all types:

    1. Student Background¶

    India has many religions, castes, and languages. In classrooms, especially urban ones, you find:

    • Students with different mother tongues
    • Different religious practices (Muslim girls wearing purdah, turbaned Sikhs, caste marks)
    • Different dress styles (traditional sari, modern churidar, gowns)

    2. Cognitive Ability¶

    Students with high cognitive abilities sit beside slow learners. This creates a challenging teaching task because:

    • Grouping by ability level is one option
    • Individualized instruction suits different cognitive levels
    • Inclusive classrooms help slow learners but may concern parents of gifted children

    3. Different Levels of Motivation¶

    Motivation varies due to:

    • Family environment — problems at home affect concentration
    • Cognitive aptitude — low ability leads to dislike of assignments
    • Attitude toward school — students who feel mistreated show withdrawal tendency

    Connection to learning: Low motivation directly affects learning achievement, which is why teachers must use reinforcement strategies (discussed in Section 2.06).

    4. Learning Difficulties¶

    Even students with average intelligence may have:

    Learning Difficulty What It Affects
    Phonological disorder Speech
    Dyslexia Reading
    Dysgraphia Writing
    Dyscalculia Mathematical computation

    These decrease learning interest and efficiency, requiring special attention (see Section 2.06:7).

    5. Diversity in Faith and Ideas¶

    Students have different religious beliefs and political thoughts. When teachers or peers impose ideas:

    • Conflict and confusion arise
    • Some students blindly support teachers to avoid displeasure
    • This affects personality development

    Important: Teachers and students should mutually respect others' rights.

    6. Diversity in Lifestyle¶

    Rural and urban students differ in habits and language command. They may not mix, remaining as separate islands, which affects learning achievement.

    7. Awareness about Cultural Differences (Positive Attitude)¶

    Diverse classrooms offer chances to interact with different backgrounds. This:

    • Widens viewpoints
    • Develops cultural awareness
    • Prevents racial, religious, and social abuses
    • Teaches acceptance and respect for other traditions

    8. Tolerance and Acceptance¶

    Working together in group activities develops:

    • Understanding of human diversities
    • Tolerance and adjustment skills
    • Ability to complete group assignments successfully

    Key Insight

    These eight types of diversity are interconnected. A student's background (Type 1) affects their motivation (Type 3). Learning difficulties (Type 4) interact with cognitive ability (Type 2). Cultural awareness (Type 7) enables tolerance (Type 8). Teachers must see the whole picture.

    2.04 Teaching in a Diverse Classroom: Differentiated Instruction¶

    In diverse classrooms, teachers must implement differentiated instruction — adapting teaching to fulfill learning needs of all students.

    There are three important aspects:

    Differentiated Instruction
         │
         ├── 1. Differentiating Content (What to learn)
         │
         ├── 2. Differentiating Process (How to learn)
         │
         └── 3. Differentiating Evaluation (How to assess)

    2.04:1 Differentiating Content to be Learned¶

    Content includes knowledge, concepts, practical skills, and positive attitudes from the curriculum.

    Methods for differentiating content delivery:

    • Demonstration
    • Audio-visual aids
    • Lecturing and explaining
    • Discussion
    • Graphical representations

    Why differentiate content? Students with different cognitive abilities (Type 2 diversity) and learning difficulties (Type 4) need information presented in different ways.

    2.04:2 Differentiating Learning Processes¶

    Learning process refers to how students understand and internalize content. Students need reflection before moving to the next segment.

    Helpful learning activities:

    Activity Purpose
    Workshop Hands-on practice and reflection
    Group Learning Collaboration and social skills
    Learning Resource Centers Extra support for challenged students

    Learning Resource Centers

    Physically challenged, mentally handicapped, and students with learning difficulties attend classroom instruction, then go to Learning Resource Centers on campus to strengthen learning with help from special education teachers.

    Group learning with mixed boys and girls promotes:

    • Social skills
    • Harmonious behaviour
    • Understanding differences among people

    Connection: This addresses the tolerance and acceptance (Type 8 diversity) — students learn to work with different people.

    2.04:3 Using Different Methods for Evaluating Learning¶

    Just as content and process are differentiated, evaluation methods must vary:

    Traditional Methods Novel Methods
    Written tests Quiz programmes
    Examinations Objective/Multiple Choice tests
    Practical examinations
    Project work evaluation
    Assignment evaluation
    Pair and share technique

    Pair and Share Technique

    Each student evaluates the learning proficiency of their partner with whom they learned a task and shared experiences. Teacher compares student evaluation with their own to finalize assessment.

    What gets evaluated: Not just subject content mastery, but also social skills, participation, and other aspects.

    2.05 Preparation of Teachers for Diverse Classrooms¶

    How do teachers learn to handle diversity? Through systematic preparation:

    Stage 1: Micro-teaching¶

    Develop individual teaching skills:

    • Set induction (introducing a topic)
    • Explaining
    • Questioning
    • Demonstration
    • Blackboard writing
    • Stimulus variation
    • Reinforcement
    • Closure

    Stage 2: Skill Integration¶

    Practice combining skills in full lessons through judicious selection.

    Stage 3: Peer Teaching and Feedback¶

    • Observe peer teachings
    • Analyze them
    • Receive professor's feedback
    • Understand various teaching techniques

    Stage 4: Diverse Classroom Exposure¶

    Visit different settings to understand diverse students:

    Type of Institution What You Learn
    Government schools Students from weaker sections
    Special schools Deaf, dumb, visually handicapped students
    Schools for mentally challenged Different learning needs
    Residential schools Community living dynamics
    International schools Alternative teaching approaches

    Stage 5: Social Awareness Activities¶

    Participate in activities during weekly holidays:

    • Visit slums and villages
    • Engage in literacy movements
    • Create health awareness
    • Promote nutritious food knowledge
    • Encourage personal hygiene

    Connection: Understanding the backgrounds students come from (Type 1 diversity) helps teachers address motivation issues (Type 3) and lifestyle differences (Type 6).

    2.06 Effective Teaching in a Diverse Classroom¶

    Eight dimensions of effective teaching address the challenges of diversity:

    2.06:1 Simplify Content and Present Effectively¶

    • Arrange concepts in logical order
    • Relate to students' prior knowledge
    • Use multimedia presentation because learning styles differ

    Some students prefer: - Teacher explaining with examples - Visual presentation - Learning by doing - Individualized learning - Group learning

    Connection to diversity: This addresses cognitive ability differences (Type 2) and learning difficulties (Type 4).

    2.06:2 Give Due Importance to Group Work¶

    For Mixed Ability Groups, avoid: - ✗ One teaching method for whole class - ✗ Teaching centered only on average learners - ✗ Teacher-centered interactions only

    Use group-based techniques:

    1. Group Interactive Classroom Sessions
    2. Cooperative Learning
    3. Collaborative Learning
    4. Group Assignments
    5. Group Investigation Learning
    6. Group Project Work

    Connection: Group work builds tolerance (Type 8) and addresses different motivation levels (Type 3).

    2.06:3 Give More Scope for Student Interaction¶

    Classroom activities fall under three categories:

    Classroom Interactions
         │
         ├── Perception about classroom climate
         │
         ├── Diagnosis
         │
         └── Action-Reaction Process (most important)

    Interaction types:

    Verbal Interactions Non-Verbal Interactions
    Teacher initiates → Students respond Related to teaching task
    Students initiate → Teacher responds Not related to teaching task

    Key principle: Student participation must be high for active learning.

    2.06:4 Provide Immediate Feedback¶

    When students complete any task (test, classroom answer, assignment, project), provide:

    • Whether task is done correctly or not
    • If correct, to what extent
    • If incorrect, why it is incorrect

    Why immediate? Enables students to get motivated, continue learning, achieve mastery, and progress with enthusiasm.

    2.06:5 Motivate Students to Actively Engage¶

    Use reinforcement: - Praise correct responses - Reward achievement - Provide immediate feedback

    Structure assignments strategically:

    Difficulty Progression:
Easy (everyone can complete) → Gradually increasing → Difficult

NOT: Difficult first → Easy later

    Principle: Initial success motivates students to try harder tasks. Success breeds success.

    Connection: This directly addresses motivation differences (Type 3 diversity).

    2.06:6 Relate Subject Content with Practical Life¶

    Use examples from day-to-day life when explaining concepts:

    • All sections of students understand more easily
    • Interest in learning increases
    • Knowledge becomes meaningful

    Connection to Part B: This principle connects to out-of-school learning — real-life applications show why learning beyond classroom matters.

    2.06:7 Pay Special Attention to Students Who Lag Behind¶

    Two methods to help struggling students:

    Method Description
    Supervised Study Teacher monitors and guides while student works
    Tutorial Method One-on-one or small group focused teaching

    2.06:8 Refer to Specialist Teachers¶

    For disabled students, connect with itinerant teachers — specialists who visit schools regularly to support specific needs.

    How the Eight Dimensions Connect

    These dimensions form a system: Simplified content (1) allows group work (2) where interactions (3) occur. Immediate feedback (4) maintains motivation (5). Real-life connections (6) help all students. Special attention (7) and specialists (8) ensure no one is left behind.

    Part B: Learning In and Out of School¶

    2.07 Concept of Out-of-School Learning¶

    The connection between diverse classrooms and out-of-school learning is this: students who struggle in traditional classrooms may learn better through direct experience in real settings.

    Historical Background¶

    Year Thinker Key Idea
    1974 Ivan Illich "Deschooling Society" — questioned whether schools are the only way to learn
    1974+ John Holt "Could schools alone serve as the best places to provide education? Or are there other alternatives?"
    1987 Lauren Resnik Popularized out-of-school learning concept at Agricultural Economics Research Association

    What is Out-of-School Learning?¶

    All experiences (academic and non-academic) that students get outside school:

    Traditional community resources: - Hospitals, engineering industries, automobile repair shops - Zoo, botanical gardens, horticultural gardens - Hotels, tailoring shops, bakeries - Libraries, science museums, cine studios

    Modern addition: Learning from home using electronic media, particularly internet (though some hesitate to include this since it's not in a social setting).

    2.07:1 Salient Features of Out-of-School Learning¶

    Feature Description
    Social settings Learning happens in community, not isolation
    Direct experiences Contact with objects and events in their own settings
    Unstructured but planned Flexible yet purposeful environments
    Collaboration Learning with others and sharing learning
    Observation emphasis Learning through watching
    Group assessment Achievement evaluated by group performance, not individual

    Connection to diverse classrooms: Students with different learning styles (a hidden diversity) may thrive in these unstructured, observation-based, collaborative settings.

    2.08 Purposes of Learning In and Out of School¶

    2.08:1 Purposes of Learning IN School¶

    Purpose What Students Develop
    Cognitive and physical skills Skills for successful daily life
    Higher-order abilities Inquiry, logical thinking, value analysis, problem-solving
    Cultural elements Language, social practices, values, knowledge, traditions, ethics, laws
    Good conduct Ethical behaviour and personal hygiene
    Vocational foundation Basic knowledge for self-reliance
    Citizenship Preparation for civic duties

    Summary: School education blooms latent potential and develops personality through structured activities. It provides "what we know".

    2.08:2 Purposes of Learning OUT OF School¶

    Society continuously changes — dress habits, food, marriage rituals, production techniques, transport, communication (material culture) as well as language, customs, beliefs, attitudes, laws (non-material culture).

    Key Insight

    Teaching today's technology and lifestyles is insufficient because they will change. Instead, develop the ability to adapt to any change — this is true education.

    What out-of-school learning provides:

    What Students Gain How They Gain It
    Problem management ability Direct experience in society
    Social skills Collaborating and sharing with others
    Adaptation skills New situations in real settings
    Life skills and problem-solving Real-life situations

    Summary: Out-of-school learning provides "what we need to know" — practical, adaptable knowledge.

    2.08:3 Comparison: Learning In School vs. Out of School¶

    Aspect In School Out of School
    Teacher's role Actively teaches, key role in learning Students take responsibility, learn through direct experience
    Evaluation focus Individual-centered activities and assessment Students share activities, work with team spirit
    Skills emphasis Intellectual and physical skills Higher-order mental skills PLUS social skills (collaboration, adjustment, adaptation)
    Knowledge type Theoretical Empirical and functional
    Retention May forget over time (except basic skills) Internalized through experience, not easily forgotten
    Scope of knowledge Wide but shallow (general skills) Narrow but deep (situation-specific skills)
    Adaptation Limited environmental adaptation Develops ability to adapt to new situations
    Learning activities Listening, seeing, reading; individual assessment Observation, personal handling, natural/social settings; group assessment

    The Connection: Both are needed. School provides breadth and foundation; out-of-school provides depth and practical application.

    2.09 Observational Learning and Its Importance¶

    2.09:1 Meaning of Observational Learning¶

    Observational learning involves:

    1. Keenly watching how others perform a task
    2. Understanding components and their arrangement
    3. Remembering these details
    4. Trying the task repeatedly
    5. Eliminating errors
    6. Achieving similar performance

    What it develops: - Language and physical skills - Moral behaviour - Social attitudes

    Role Model and Modelling

    The person we watch and try to imitate is our role model. Learning by observation is called modelling. Teachers and parents must behave as good role models.

    2.09:1:01 Steps in Observational Learning (Bandura)¶

    Step Process Description
    1 Attending and Perceiving Child's attention is attracted by role model or demonstration
    2 Remembering Child remembers actions and behaviour observed
    3 Converting memory to action Child analyzes acceptability and imitates only relevant, acceptable elements
    4 Reinforcement Only behaviours that receive appropriate feedback become fixed

    Key insight: Children don't imitate everything — they select what seems relevant and acceptable. Reinforcement determines what sticks.

    2.09:2 Importance in Out-of-School Learning¶

    Why classroom learning is insufficient:

    • Cannot provide all information and practical uses
    • Functional knowledge requires natural and social environments
    • Practical skills need real settings

    This is why educators like John Dewey advocated project work.

    Applications:

    Setting How Observational Learning Helps
    Vocational education Apprenticeship under professional experts/supervisors
    Fine arts academies Learning by watching master artists
    Technical training centers Observing experienced workers
    Personal interests Pursuing passions through observation
    Learning difficulties Alternative paths when classroom fails

    Components of observational learning: - Observing objects, events, acts in natural settings - Gathering appropriate information - Interpreting collected data - Analyzing, classifying, synthesizing

    In apprenticeship: - Observe efficient workers - Identify components and sequence - Note perfection achieved - Retain in memory - Try performing - Receive supervisor feedback - Practice until errors disappear and speed increases

    2.10 Approaches to Learning Outside the Classroom¶

    2.10:1 Modern View¶

    Traditional view: Learn in social settings using community resources (hospitals, gardens, museums, workshops, etc.)

    Modern view: Internet-based learning from home. Access any website to learn about: - Preparing food recipes - Yoga, music, dance - Science experiments - Machine assembly and repair - World parks and famous buildings - Archaeological findings - Space research

    Three approaches to out-of-school learning:

    Out-of-School Learning Approaches
              │
              ├── 1. Field Training (Field Trip)
              │
              ├── 2. Field Research
              │
              └── 3. Web-based Learning

    2.10:2 Field Training (Field Trip)¶

    Definition: Training designed for students to get full sensory experiences with things and phenomena that cannot be brought into the classroom.

    Three Phases:

    Phase Activities
    Pre-field Teacher outlines key concepts, asks students to study topic, displays related video/slides
    Field Work Make arrangements, reach location, give directions about what to observe and record, students collect information and interpretations
    Post-field Discussion of observations, individual reports (diagrams, stories, poems, essays), teacher evaluation and feedback

    Benefits: - Students report observations with their own interpretations - Even students without personal experience learn from shared notes - Direct experiences through structured situations

    Using Field Trip as Teaching Aid:

    1. Preview the place after determining purpose
    2. Make arrangements with school and site authorities
    3. Prepare pupils physically and mentally — make trip feel essential and interesting, clarify what to look for
    4. Function as prudent guide — allow students to observe, take notes, draw sketches, collect materials
    5. Follow-up work — discussions, readings, exhibitions, laboratory verification
    6. Evaluate in terms of specific educational gains

    2.10:3 Field Research¶

    Steps involved:

    Step Activity
    1. Sensing the Problem From teacher explanation, personal observation, or interest, select problem for solution
    2. Formulating Hypothesis Through critical thinking, reading, discussion, propose possible solution statement
    3. Field Activities Collect required data to test hypothesis
    4. Data Analysis Process raw data, classify, tabulate, make statistical analysis
    5. Hypothesis Testing Accept or reject hypothesis based on findings
    6. Write-up Prepare short report of problem-solving method

    Methods used: Inductive and deductive reasoning

    Skills developed: Observation, information collection, description, explanation — all centered on problem-solving using scientific method.

    Connection: Field research applies the problem-solving skills that school develops, in real-world settings.

    2.10:4 Learning Through Websites¶

    Steps involved:

    Step Activity
    1 Search websites using search engines (Google)
    2 Select appropriate websites, surf using browser
    3 Find relevant information, download and store as file
    4 Print out; share via email if needed
    5 Process, arrange sequentially, prepare report with text and pictures

    2.11 Advantages of Learning Outside the Classroom¶

    2.11:1 Advantages of Field Trip and Field Research¶

    1. First-hand quality experiences — natural phenomena in proper background
    2. Close contact with environment
    3. Develops observation and reasoning skills
    4. Encourages active learning
    5. Socializes school activities — school and community get closer
    6. Materials collection for school museum/exhibition
    7. Develops social skills
    8. Opportunities for collaborative learning and sharing
    9. Makes knowledge meaningful and enlarges it
    10. Develops problem-solving skills

    2.11:2 Advantages of Web-based Learning¶

    1. Develops ability to derive meaning and engage in active discourse
    2. Encourages self-construction of knowledge instead of receiving tailor-made information
    3. Enables mutual sharing between teacher and students
    4. Learner-centred
    5. Encourages active participation and knowledge construction
    6. Fosters higher-order cognitive abilities — analysis, synthesis, evaluation
    7. Encourages active learning
    8. Allows group learning — develops cooperation and understanding
    9. Enables interaction at different levels
    10. Focuses on real-world events and problem-solving

    2.12 Limitations of Learning Outside the Classroom¶

    Challenges in Field Trips and Field Research¶

    Challenge Description
    Supervision Large numbers of students need help
    Scheduling Difficulty framing time table
    Missed lessons Hard to arrange make-up sessions
    Logistics Challenging to arrange and conduct
    Cost Travel and stay expenses
    Knowledge gaps May not know enough about field location
    Safety Ensuring student safety
    Skill gaps Most students lack ability to collect and interpret information
    Attitude Danger of treating trip as excursion/pleasure tour

    Challenges in Web-based Learning¶

    Challenge Description
    Computer skills Students may lack ability to operate computers, search, collect information
    Language barrier About 90% of web information is in English — challenging for rural students
    Interpretation Lack ability to properly interpret and select from collected materials
    Technical skills Cannot download and record to compact discs
    Access Not all students have computer and internet
    Reporting Cannot process, arrange logically, and prepare written reports

    Reality in India

    Language skills, computer skills, information selection and interpretation, logical arrangement and report writing — adequate practice and proficiency may be lacking in Indian students. This makes 'learning outside school' techniques appear as a rare feat.

    Unit Summary

    Part A established that classrooms contain students with eight types of diversity (background, cognitive ability, motivation, learning difficulties, faith/ideas, lifestyle, cultural awareness, tolerance). To address this, teachers use differentiated instruction across content, process, and evaluation. Effective teaching involves eight dimensions from simplifying content to using specialists.

    Part B showed that learning extends beyond school walls. Out-of-school learning provides direct experience, observation-based learning, and social skills that complement school's theoretical foundation. Observational learning (Bandura's four steps) enables learning through role models. Three approaches — field trips, field research, and web-based learning — each have specific advantages and limitations.

    The connection: Diverse students need diverse learning environments. Combining differentiated classroom instruction with meaningful out-of-school experiences ensures every student can learn effectively.
