    Unit 1: Teaching and Learning — Nature and Concepts¶

    1.00 Introduction¶

    Though learning is universal and applicable to all living organisms, humans have superior learning abilities. Because of this, humans can:

    • Control their environment and other organisms
    • Speak and communicate
    • Acquire fine motor abilities
    • Own culture and practise different arts and crafts
    • Form concepts using symbols
    • Use language in thinking

    The learning ability of each individual depends on their level of intelligence. Learning is constantly taking place in all aspects of human life.

    How This Unit is Organized:

    This unit builds understanding progressively:

    1. First, we understand what learning is (definitions, elements, nature)
    2. Then, we explore what affects learning (factors, principles)
    3. Next, we compare types of learning (rote vs meaningful, passive vs active, self-learning)
    4. Finally, we examine teaching — because teaching exists to facilitate learning

    The Core Connection: Everything in this unit revolves around one idea — learning is a permanent change in behaviour through experience, and teaching is the system of actions designed to make that change happen.

    Part A: Understanding Learning¶

    1.01 Meaning of Learning¶

    Learning can be understood in three ways:

    Perspective What Learning Means
    Acquisition Getting knowledge, habits, attitudes, and practical skills through study, self-experiences, or being taught
    Modification Changing one's behaviour through practice, training, and experiences
    Adaptation Adjusting behaviour to one's environment

    Simple Definition

    Learning is what one gains as knowledge, habits, attitudes, and practical skills either through reading, self-experiences, or teaching by others.

    1.02 Definitions of Learning¶

    Scholar Definition
    Angela Single Acquiring any skill that enriches your life — through your own exploration, through sharing, or by instruction from anyone
    Kristi McGrath Accumulation of our experiences and the consequential growth and new understanding of the world around us
    Thomas Correll A life-long process of gaining and using information. Learning is successful only when the information gained is used and understood
    Stephen B. Klein A relatively permanent change in behaviour that results from experience

    Common Agreement Among Psychologists

    Learning is a relatively permanent change in an individual's behaviour and attitude, over time, based upon training and experiences.

    Why this matters: Notice that all definitions emphasize change and experience. This connects directly to the elements of learning discussed next.

    1.03 Important Elements of Learning¶

    Analyzing the definitions reveals three essential elements:

    Elements of Learning
        │
        ├── 1. PROCESS
        │       └── Acquiring knowledge, skills, attitudes through effort
        │
        ├── 2. EFFECT
        │       └── Producing behaviour modification
        │
        └── 3. NATURE OF EFFECT
                └── Behaviour modification lasts at least to some extent

    What is NOT Learning:

    Change Type Why It's Not Learning
    Changes from instincts Not caused by experience or training
    Changes from maturation Natural development, not learning
    Fatigue-induced changes Temporary, not permanent
    Disease-induced changes Not from training/experience
    Medicine-induced changes Short-term, not lasting

    Connection: These elements explain WHY the factors and principles of learning (Sections 1.05 and 1.06) matter — they all contribute to the process, effect, or permanence of behaviour change.

    1.04 Nature of Learning¶

    Learning has six key characteristics:

    # Characteristic Explanation Connection to Other Concepts
    1 Universal All living beings learn But humans have superior learning (intro)
    2 Continuous Takes place from "womb to tomb" This is why life-long learning matters (self-learning, Section 1.14)
    3 Results in Improved Performance Learning leads to better outcomes This is the "effect" element (Section 1.03)
    4 Purposive Linked to goals, purposes, satisfactions No one learns without purpose — connects to motivation (Section 1.05)
    5 Multiple and Integrative Combines verbal, perceptual, motor, conceptual, problem-solving, and emotional learning This is why different teaching methods suit different learners
    6 Contingent upon Experience Based on self-experience, not just given information This connects to active learning (Section 1.09) and self-learning (Section 1.14)

    Learning is Integrative — An Example

    A girl learning shorthand doesn't just learn a motor skill. She simultaneously learns:

    • Verbal learning: Arbitrary relationships between visual symbols
    • Motor learning: How to make the symbols
    • Associative learning: How to apply the symbols
    • Emotional learning: Attitudes about shorthand, the commercial world, and herself

    Key Insight: The integrative nature of learning explains why teaching methods must also be integrative — addressing knowledge, skills, AND attitudes together.

    1.05 Factors Influencing Learning¶

    Understanding what affects learning helps teachers design better instruction. These factors connect to specific teaching strategies.

    Internal Factors (Within the Learner)¶

    Factor How It Influences Learning Teaching Implication
    Motivation Driving force necessary for learning (McConnell) Use reinforcement, make content relevant
    Needs Environmental needs stimulate learning; more needs = more learning Connect learning to student needs
    Maturation Provides necessary learning readiness Don't teach before students are ready
    Intelligence Determines learning method, rate, and amount Differentiate instruction
    Attention Pre-requisite for learning Use stimulus variation, audio-visual aids
    Aptitude Learning becomes easier when field suits aptitude Consider student interests
    Attitudes Positive attitudes make learning interesting; negative attitudes reduce interest Build positive learning environment

    External Factors (In the Environment)¶

    Factor How It Influences Learning Teaching Implication
    Feedback Accelerates and consolidates learning Provide immediate feedback (Active Learning technique)
    Audio-Visual Aids Attracts and retains attention Use multimedia presentation
    Guidance Enhances security and self-confidence Though learning is individual, guidance helps
    Learning Environment Warm, pleasant atmosphere is highly helpful Create conducive classroom climate
    Distributed Practice Enhances effectiveness Space out learning sessions
    Rewards & Punishments Play significant roles in motivation Use positive reinforcement
    Initial Success Induces further learning Start with achievable tasks
    Perceptual Learning Understanding relations between elements makes tasks easier Help students see the whole picture
    Correlation with Past Knowledge Leads to meaningful learning Connect new content to prior knowledge

    Summary

    Factors affecting learning include: (i) nature of learners' potentials, needs, and learning ability (ii) learning environments (iii) qualities and abilities of teacher (iv) methods of learning (v) opportunities and facilities available (vi) motivation

    Connection: These factors explain WHY the principles of learning (next section) work — each principle addresses one or more of these factors.

    1.06 Basic Principles of Learning¶

    These principles guide effective teaching practice. Each connects to the factors and elements already discussed.

    # Principle Explanation Connection
    1 Learning is Growth Physical growth + training + experience = development Links maturation (factor) with experience (element)
    2 Adjustment to Environment Purpose is to adapt oneself — suitable response to given stimulus This is the "adaptation" meaning of learning (1.01)
    3 Organizing Experiences Integration of new and existing experiences; refining accumulated knowledge Connects to "correlation with past knowledge" factor
    4 Learning is Purposeful Effective only when it satisfies goals and desires Connects to "purposive" nature (1.04) and motivation factor
    5 Learning is an Intelligent Act Understanding components and relations improves performance This is gaining "insight" — leads to meaningful learning
    6 Active Participation "Learning by doing" — requires self-motivation Foundation for Active Learning (Section 1.09)
    7 Individual and Social Self-motivated but influenced by family, peer group, school, movies, places of worship Balance between self-learning and social learning
    8 Cannot be Forced Must be undertaken willingly; requires proper preparation Emphasizes motivation factor
    9 Environmental Interaction Requires conducive environment; doesn't happen in vacuum Connects to learning environment factor
    10 Behaviour Change Goal is behaviour modification and improvement This is the "effect" element (1.03)
    11 Wholeness (Gestalt) Integration makes learning meaningful; see totality not parts Connects to "multiple and integrative" nature
    12 Insight-Based Sudden understanding of components and relationships Depends on previous experience, intelligence, problem presentation

    1.07 Implications of Principles of Learning¶

    What the principles mean for teaching practice:

    1. Meaningful learning is more effective than rote learning — makes learning easier and leads to further learning

    2. Meaningful learning enhances involvement and participation — students engage more deeply

    3. Content learned meaningfully lasts longer — addresses the "permanence" element

    4. Ability to read and understand leads to self-learning — connects to self-learning (Section 1.14)

    5. Individual differences exist in learning — teachers must organize instruction accordingly

    Accommodating Different Learning Styles¶

    Learning Style Preferred Activities
    Auditory learners Interview, debate, discussion, lectures
    Visual learners Diagrams, cartoons, charts, films, computer graphics
    Tactile learners Models, embossed letters and pictures (for visually handicapped)
    Analytical learners Sequentially presented matter
    Global learners Computer programs, documented books, lessons in story form

    Connection to Part B: This understanding of different learners leads us to examine different types of learning — rote vs meaningful, passive vs active.

    Part B: Types of Learning¶

    1.08 Rote Learning and Meaningful Learning¶

    The principles of learning indicate that meaningful learning is superior. But what exactly is the difference?

    Definitions¶

    Type What It Means
    Rote Learning Reproduction of learned material verbatim; learner doesn't check if material is correct or incorrect
    Meaningful Learning Learners understand and internalize matter; express it in their own language; output is more than input

    1.08:1 Comparison Table¶

    Aspect Rote Learning Meaningful Learning
    Involvement No full involvement Full involvement
    Output Repeated verbatim Expressed in own style
    Participation Non-participatory process Full participation
    Recall Fragmented pieces Letter and spirit
    Interest Decreases over time Increases more and more
    Independence Fully depends on teacher Increases independent functioning
    Output vs Input Less than input Equal or more than input

    Analogy

    Rote learners are like parasites — incapable of giving fruits.

    Meaningful learners are like lush green plants — growing independently with capacity to bear fruits.

    The Connection: Rote learners repeat like parrots. Meaningful learners enrich what they've learned with imagination and present it in novel ways. This leads directly to the concept of active learning — where students don't just receive but actively construct meaning.

    1.09 Active Learning¶

    The concept of 'Active Learning' was first introduced by R.W. Revans.

    What Makes Learning "Active"?¶

    If learning involves not only hearing but also: - Reading - Writing - Discussing - Problem-solving - Direct involvement and experience

    ...then it is Active Learning.

    This is the opposite of passive learning — where students sit silently and receive information from the teacher.

    Active Learning includes all three domains: - Knowledge - Skills - Attitudes

    1.09:1 Meaning of Active Learning¶

    Definition

    Active Learning is the learning process in which students get directly involved and learn by experience — involving themselves in meaningful learning activities and thinking about what they are doing.

    Alternative names: Play-way learning, Technology-based learning, Group learning, Project method

    1.09:2 Definition of Active Learning¶

    Scholar Definition
    Bonwell & Eison (1991) "Anything that involves students in doing things and thinking about the things they are doing"
    Weltman "A method of learning in which students are actively or experientially involved in the learning process. There are different levels depending on student-involvement"
    Felder & Brent (2009) "Anything course-related that all students in a class session are called upon to do other than simply watching, listening and taking notes"

    Two key aspects: 1) Students doing learning activities 2) Thinking about what they're doing

    Connection: Active learning builds on the principle that "learning is active participation" (Principle 6) and addresses the factor that "learning is contingent upon experience" (Nature 6).

    1.10 Principles of Active Learning¶

    Five important principles explain WHY active learning works:

    # Principle Explanation Connection
    1 Active Construction of Meaning Learner examines new information against existing knowledge; if contradicting, old information is removed and new conceptualization forms Connects to "organizing experiences" principle
    2 Learning Facts ≠ Learning to Do Students can know facts but be unable to apply them; practice and feedback needed Connects to "intelligent act" principle — understanding relations
    3 Transfer of Learning Some learning is domain-specific; other learning transfers to other situations Developing transfer skills is important
    4 Social Learning Individuals learn more with others than alone Connects to "individual and social" principle
    5 Articulating Explanations Speaking/writing key concepts helps learn them effectively Oral repetition and writing aid understanding

    Additional Concepts from Educators:

    • Frequent student-faculty contacts encourage motivation
    • Collaborative learning promotes meaningful learning
    • Getting feedback facilitates meaningful learning — when students understand what they have and haven't learned, they can concentrate and enrich their learning

    1.11 Nature or Characteristics of Active Learning¶

    Barnes (1989) suggested seven characteristics:

    Characteristic Description Why It Matters
    Purposive Relevance of task to meaningful learning Connects to "learning is purposeful" principle
    Reflective Students reflect on meaning of what is learned Deep processing, not surface memorization
    Negotiated Goals and methods negotiated between teachers and students Student involvement in planning
    Critical Students critically analyze ways of learning Metacognition — thinking about thinking
    Complex Comparing tasks with real-life complexities Authentic learning experiences
    Situation-driven Tasks selected according to situational needs Relevant, contextual learning
    Engaged Real-life tasks reflected in learning activities Student involvement increases

    Connection: These characteristics show how active learning addresses multiple factors influencing learning — motivation, environment, relevance, and feedback.

    1.12 Techniques of Active Learning¶

    Practical methods to implement active learning:

    Core Techniques¶

    Technique Description Purpose
    Think-Pair-Share Students ponder answer, then share with neighbour Reflection + Social learning
    Role Playing Look at topic from character's perspective (e.g., being affected by earthquake, pollution) Emotional engagement + Multiple perspectives
    Jigsaw Puzzle Carving pieces, then joining appropriately to know components Understanding parts and wholes
    Peer Review Review and comment on classmates' work (essays, models) Critical analysis + Feedback
    Discussion Properly framed questions promoting higher-order thinking Deep processing + Social learning
    Problem-solving with Real Data Use variety of data to explore scientific questions Application of knowledge
    Game-Based Learning Competitive exercises or computer simulations Engagement through play

    1.13 Implications of Facilitating Active Learning¶

    Active learning techniques can be implemented at individual level without affecting regular class flow. These exercises help instructors know how far students understand and retain content.

    1.13:1 Technique of 'One Minute Paper'¶

    Purpose: Test how far students have understood classroom teaching

    Process: 1. At end of class, students take out blank paper 2. Teacher asks important question (open or closed) related to day's lesson 3. Students think (1-2 minutes) and answer within a page

    Examples: - What is the central idea of the lesson taught today? - What are the five important components of scientific attitude? - What is an exothermic chemical reaction? - What is meant by 'Self-learning'?

    Connection: Provides immediate feedback (a key factor in learning).

    1.13:2 Making to Report Affective Response¶

    Similar to One Minute Paper, but: - Students report emotional reactions instead of factual answers - Suitable for language and social science (not mathematics/science)

    Examples: - Describe the feeling expressed by Wordsworth in the sonnet 'Daffodils' - State your reactions after reading about the massacre at Jallianwala Bagh

    Connection: Addresses emotional learning — part of the integrative nature of learning.

    1.13:3 Daily Assignment¶

    More time given for deeper thinking (10 minutes in class or home assignment)

    Drawback: Teacher cannot get immediate feedback

    Advantage: Can ask more complex questions

    Example: Point out any two research findings showing the importance of heredity in personality development.

    1.13:4 Reading Quiz¶

    Purpose: Ensure students come prepared for class

    Process: 1. Assign reading of next lesson 2. At beginning of class, assess preparedness through one-word answer questions 3. Guide students on what to look for when reading

    Connection: Active learning depends upon students coming prepared.

    1.13:5 Clarification Pauses¶

    Process: 1. After explaining difficult concepts, teacher provides a long pause 2. Teacher goes around classroom, looks at students' notes 3. Provides clarifications for individual doubts

    Advantage: Students shy of expressing doubts openly can get clarification privately.

    1.13:6 Socratic Method¶

    Origin: Used by Greek philosopher Socrates

    Process: 1. Teacher asks question to test knowledge 2. If student can't answer correctly, pass to next student 3. Continue until correct answer is elicited

    Connection: Question-answer method promotes active thinking.

    1.13:7 Student Summary¶

    Purpose: Make all students attentive to classmates' answers

    Process: 1. After one student answers 2. Another student is asked to summarize that answer

    Problem addressed: When one student answers, others often don't listen attentively.

    1.13:8 Fish Bowl Method¶

    Process: 1. Each student gets index card with their name 2. At end of class, each writes two difficult questions about the content 3. Deposit in plastic box 4. Teacher lists questions 5. Next class, teacher reads questions and elicits answers from students 6. If no one can answer, teacher explains in detail

    Outcome: Ensures active participation leading to meaningful learning.

    1.13:9 Finger Signal¶

    Process: 1. Teacher poses multiple choice questions with four answers 2. Students indicate answer by raising fingers (1 finger = answer 1, etc.) 3. Teacher quickly sees who understood and who didn't

    Example: Is carbon present in all organic compounds? - 1) Yes 2) No 3) Sometimes 4) Most of the times

    1.13:10 Flash Cards¶

    Alternative to Finger Signal: 1. Teacher flashes cards with questions (10 seconds each) 2. Students respond with raised fingers 3. Cards with poor responses are kept separately 4. At end, teacher shows these cards and explains correct answers with reasons

    1.13:11 Share Learning in Pair¶

    Simple form of group learning: - Students paired for sharing learning activities - Avoids difficulties of forming larger groups - Eliminates non-contribution by group members

    1.13:12 Discussion (Paired)¶

    Process: 1. Students paired and sitting in circle 2. For questions asked, pairs consult each other 3. Can combine with other techniques (questions/answers, flash cards)

    1.13:13 Evaluation of Another Student's Work¶

    Process: 1. Class divided into pairs for whole semester 2. Each student prepares assignment — one copy to teacher, one to pair-mate 3. Each evaluates partner's assignment and provides feedback 4. Teacher compares student assessment with own assessment for final grade

    Benefit: Students analyze from different angles, expanding knowledge.

    Connection to Part C: These active learning techniques contrast with passive learning and prepare students for independent self-learning.

    Part C: Self-Learning¶

    1.14 Self-Learning¶

    Active learning leads naturally to self-learning — the ability to learn independently.

    1.14:1 Meaning of Self-Learning¶

    Definition

    Self-Learning is learning without the assistance of a teacher or instructor — learning with one's own efforts at home.

    Also known as: Self-teaching, Self-instruction, Auto-instruction

    Key points: - Anyone can learn any curriculum without help from others - Structured content (e.g., programmed learning materials) is suitable - Motivated learners can learn any subject matter with self-effort - Self-learners can learn course work in much less time than those learning with teacher help

    1.14:2 Definition of Self-Learning¶

    Formal Definition

    Self-learning is collecting information by one's own efforts, processing and understanding them without the assistance of others, and retaining them in memory.

    Characteristics of Self-Learners¶

    Characteristic Explanation
    High self-motivation Gather information through own efforts
    High self-confidence Access different resources even for abstract/difficult matter
    Sense of satisfaction Finishing tasks efficiently motivates more learning

    Connection: These characteristics connect to internal factors of learning (motivation, attitudes) and the principle that learning requires active participation.

    1.14:3 Importance of Self-Learning¶

    # Why Self-Learning Matters
    1 Understanding how to learn
    2 Essential for life-long learning
    3 Cannot always rely on external help for everything in life
    4 Know something about every field AND everything about specific field — leads to success in personal and financial matters
    5 High self-motivation and self-confidence lead to success in day-to-day life (including business)

    Connection: Recall that learning is "continuous" (Nature characteristic) — from womb to tomb. Self-learning makes this continuous learning possible.

    1.14:4 Benefits of Self-Learning¶

    # Benefit Explanation
    1 Independent thinking Ability to think for oneself
    2 Accept responsibility Learn to be responsible
    3 Freedom Learn without restrictions
    4 Accountability Realize only they are responsible for success/failure
    5 Intrinsic motivation Act for mental satisfaction, not prizes or praises
    6 High confidence Always confident to succeed in any test
    7 Better retention Retain longer than those who learn from parents/teachers
    8 Resourcefulness Know where to go when help needed; can fill educational gaps later
    9 Initiative Courage to study areas of interest without waiting for teacher
    10 Preparation for college College requires self-motivation and ability to plan ahead
    11 Good work ethics Self-learning promotes these
    12 Depth control Freedom to decide study depth based on capacity and interests
    13 Focus Restrict areas of interest; energy not dissipated
    14 Family benefits Family becomes institution to nurture relationships, not teaching
    15 No excuses Eliminates excuses for not achieving innate abilities
    16 Joyful learning More enjoyable than being taught by somebody
    17 Parent relief Mothers freed from reading textbooks to help children
    18 Original sources Access original sources; reduces mistakes in learning materials
    19 Future preparation Useful information available now and at fingertips
    20 Mother's time Can pay more attention to small kids and toddlers

    Connection to Teaching: Self-learning is the ultimate goal of good teaching — creating independent learners. This leads us to Part D: Understanding Teaching.

    Part D: Understanding Teaching¶

    1.15 Meaning and Definitions of Teaching¶

    We've explored learning in depth. Now we examine teaching — because teaching exists to facilitate learning.

    1.15:1 Meaning of Teaching¶

    In simple terms, teaching refers to: - A profession of a group called teachers - The activities undertaken to help individuals acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values

    However, teaching is more complex: - A social, cultural, and ethical process designed in social/cultural context - Highly flexible and dynamic — adapts to changes in social structure, cultural environment, ideals, values, and government policies

    Connection: Just as learning is shaped by many factors (Section 1.05), teaching is shaped by social and policy factors.

    1.15:2 Definitions Offered for Teaching¶

    Scholar Definition Key Insight
    H.C. Morrison "Teaching is an intimate contact between a more mature personality and a less mature one which is designed to further the education of the latter" Teaching as relationship
    B.O. Smith (1960) "A system of actions intended to produce learning" Teaching aims at learning
    Clark (1970) "Activities that are designed and performed to produce change in student behaviour" Connects to behaviour change element
    Benjamin Bloom Activities like lecture, question-answer, discussion, discovery, assignments aiming at cognitive, affective, and psycho-motor goals Three domains of learning
    N.L. Gage (1969) "A form of interpersonal influence aimed at changing the behaviour potential of another person" Teaching as influence
    B.O. Smith (1963) "A system of actions involving an agent, an end in view, and a situation including factors the agent cannot control (class size, physical characteristics) and those that can be modified (ways of asking questions, structuring information)" Teaching has controllable and uncontrollable factors
    Thomas F. Green "The task of a teacher which is performed for the development of a child" Teaching for development

    Thomas F. Green's Important Argument¶

    In his book "Activities in Teaching," Green argued:

    "There is no learning without teaching, and teaching may not always result in learning."

    Explanation: - Teaching intent may be learning, but all teaching may not facilitate learning - Like a lawyer pleading a case — purpose is to win, but no surety of winning - Like a doctor treating a patient — purpose is to cure, but every patient is not cured - Teacher designs teaching to induce learning, but every student may not learn

    Key insight: The purpose of teaching is to create appropriate situations which facilitate learning. Thus: No learning is possible without teaching, and all teaching may not result in learning.

    Connection: This explains why understanding learning (Part A) is essential before understanding teaching — teaching success depends on understanding how learning works.

    1.16 Nature and Characteristics of Teaching¶

    # Characteristic Explanation
    i Social activity Only teachers with special professional qualification should undertake it
    ii Observable Quality judged through teacher activities and student-teacher interactions
    iii Evaluable Can be assessed by observing it
    iv Involves feedback Teaching includes giving and receiving feedback
    v Analysable Based on types of teacher-activities, educational objectives, learning conditions, learning components
    vi Linguistic process Communication through language
    vii Therapeutic May serve as therapy for learners
    viii Prescriptive Provides guidelines/prescription for students to follow
    ix Three functions 1) Telling and listening 2) Showing/displaying 3) Demonstrating/doing
    x Interactive Face-to-face encounter
    xi Continuum From conditioning (lower) to indoctrinating (higher)
    xii Art and Science Both views acceptable
    xiii Three senses Knowledge, Profession, Process

    Teaching as a Continuum¶

    Lower Level                                            Higher Level
     │                                                      │
     ▼                                                      ▼
Conditioning → Training → Instructing → Educating → Indoctrinating
     │            │           │             │              │
  Knowledge   Skills    Skills &      Knowledge,       Beliefs &
  Learning   Learning   Behaviour     Skills &         Thinking
                                      Behaviour

    Teaching: Art or Science?¶

    As an Art As a Science
    Requires special skills Can be developed through training
    Involves constructive thoughts Based on teaching methods and techniques
    Unique to each teacher Follows established competencies

    Silverman (1966): Both views are acceptable.

    Three Senses of Teaching¶

    Sense Meaning
    Knowledge Methods, strategies, assumptions about how learning takes place; knowledge about students
    Profession Occupation of giving training and imparting education
    Process Ways of making something known to others, usually in school routine

    1.17 Characteristics of Good Teaching¶

    Good teaching is the main criterion of an effective teacher. Three types of characteristics:

    • Presage factors — Teacher background and preparation
    • Process factors — Teaching effect and interactions
    • Product factors — Student outcomes

    Good Teaching in Different Contexts¶

    Context Good Teaching Means
    Totalitarian state Teacher uses personal interaction to influence learners toward valuing state goals; what's good for state is good for individual
    Democratic state Provides freedom for learners to express ideas freely; urges them to adjust and assimilate their own values, thought patterns, and total configuration of knowledge

    Characteristics of Good Teaching in a Democratic Society¶

    # Characteristic Connection to Learning Concepts
    1 Suits modern time, social conditions, and learner abilities Addresses individual differences
    2 Less teacher control, more self-learning by students; indirect teacher behaviour Promotes self-learning (Section 1.14)
    3 Well-managed classroom focused on learning business Conducive environment (factor)
    4 Warm human environment respecting individual dignity Learning environment factor
    5 Supportive interpersonal relationships; shared problem-solving attitude Social learning principle
    6 Well-educated, mature teacher with insight and energy; fosters exploration and self-initiative Teacher quality factor
    7 Accounts for individual differences; provides variety of learning experiences Different learning styles (Section 1.07)
    8 Shows concern and care for student problems Emotional support
    9 Stimulates student interest using instructional aids Attention factor; audio-visual aids factor

    Summary: Attributes of Good Teaching¶

    • Providing opportunities for pupil's self-learning
    • Providing variety of learning experiences
    • Taking care of individual differences
    • Enhancing human relationships
    • Facilitating direct, first-hand experiences
    • Developing reflective thinking and practical skills
    • Developing humanism and problem-solving ability
    • Relevant to modern time
    • Strong teacher-pupil relationship
    • Includes formative assessment (questions during instruction) and summative assessment (review at end through Q&A)

    Unit Summary

    Part A established that learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour through experience, with three elements (process, effect, permanence) and six characteristics. Multiple internal and external factors influence learning, leading to twelve principles that guide effective practice.

    Part B distinguished types of learning: rote (passive, dependent) versus meaningful (active, independent). Active learning involves doing AND thinking, with specific techniques like Think-Pair-Share, Socratic Method, and Fish Bowl. Self-learning represents the highest form — independent, lifelong, resourceful learning.

    Part C showed that self-learning develops when learners have high motivation, confidence, and satisfaction from completing tasks. Twenty benefits include independent thinking, better retention, and preparation for lifelong learning.

    Part D explained that teaching exists to facilitate learning. It is both art and science, with characteristics including being social, observable, and evaluable. Good teaching in a democratic society provides self-learning opportunities, addresses individual differences, creates warm environments, and uses both formative and summative assessment.

    The connection: Everything flows from the core concept — learning is behaviour change through experience. Teaching creates those experiences. The best teaching develops self-directed, meaningful, active learners who can continue learning throughout life.
